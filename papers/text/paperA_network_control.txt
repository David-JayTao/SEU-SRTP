Efficient and scalable reinforcement learning 
for large-scale network control
Chengdong Ma1,2,6, Aming Li2,3,6, Yali Du4,6, Hao Dong5 & Yaodong Yang    1,2 
The primary challenge in the development of large-scale artificial 
intelligence (AI) systems lies in achieving scalable decision-making—
extending the AI models while maintaining sufficient performance.  
Existing research indicates that distributed AI can improve scalability  
by decomposing complex tasks and distributing them across collaborative 
nodes. However, previous technologies suffered from compromised 
real-world applicability and scalability due to the massive requirement 
of communication and sampled data. Here we develop a model-based 
decentralized policy optimization framework, which can be efficiently 
deployed in multi-agent systems. By leveraging local observation  
through the agent-level topological decoupling of global dynamics,  
we prove that this decentralized mechanism achieves accurate  
estimations of global information. Importantly, we further introduce  
model learning to reinforce the optimal policy for monotonic improvement 
with a limited amount of sampled data. Empirical results on diverse 
scenarios show the superior scalability of our approach, particularly in 
real-world systems with hundreds of agents, thereby paving the way for 
scaling up AI systems.
Achieving scalable decision-making becomes a critical challenge when 
deploying artificial intelligence (AI) models into large-scale systems 1. 
First, this requires effective information exchange among system enti-
ties to help an agent perceive the state of the environment and other 
agents2. However, owing to constraints and high costs associated 
with communication3, achieving comprehensive information sharing 
across the entire system becomes unfeasible (Fig. 1a). For example, 
in traffic networks, frequent and extensive communication between 
traffic lights can cause a substantial power loss (Fig. 1b). In some sys-
tems involving user information, centralized information collection 
also increases the risk of privacy leakage 4. Second, efficient sample 
acquisition and learning from limited data are necessary because the 
cost of agent–environment interaction rises exponentially with the 
scale of the system5, and in some scenarios, only limited interactions 
are permitted. Therefore, developing an effective communication 
mode and a sample-efficient approach is crucial for achieving  
scalable decision-making.
As an advanced paradigm of distributed AI, multi-agent reinforce-
ment learning (MARL) provides a possible solution6,7 and has made 
progress in various scenarios, including autonomous driving8–12, wire-
less communications13,14, multi-player games15,16, power systems17,18 and 
urban transportation19–24. The advantage of MARL lies in its ability to 
perform nonlinear fitting solely by data and attain efficient inference. 
In contrast, traditional methods such as model predictive control (MPC) 
require precise system dynamics25,26, which are often difficult to obtain 
in complex systems. Even with accurate dynamics, traditional methods 
often rely on system linearization27, which inevitably harms performance 
by disregarding numerous intricate nonlinear factors and system per-
turbations inherent in complex systems. In addition, deficiencies in 
computational efficiency, numerical stability and communication costs

Furthermore, high sample efficiency is also crucial yet unad -
dressed, rendering current methods effective in only small-scale or sim-
ulated environments16,48–50. Model-based methods are widely studied as 
a promising approach for improving sample efficiency51–56, as they can 
leverage estimated dynamic models to execute efficient interactions in 
for acquisition of policy learning data52,57. This is particularly helpful for 
the application of MARL in large-scale systems with constrained inter-
actions (Fig. 1b). However, previous research on model-based MARL 
is primarily restricted to specific scenarios, for example, two-player 
zero-sum games 58,59, the pursuit-evasion game 60 or tabular tasks 61. 
Some works formulate opponent models or dynamics in a decentral-
ized structure62, but these approaches assume full-state observability, 
and their scalability has not been sufficiently verified. Subsequently, 
some researchers extended model-based prioritized sweeping to a 
MARL scenario61 but were limited to only the tabular MARL setting, 
and thus were unable to address more general tasks. In summary, it is 
difficult to achieve scalable decision-making with existing model-based 
MARL methods owing to the following limitations63. (1) Despite being 
rooted in local models, they still rely on a global critic, undermining 
their effectiveness in communication-constrained scenarios64–66. (2) 
Theoretical analysis and bounded estimation for multi-agent model 
learning are lacking, resulting in an insufficient understanding of the 
model’s impact on MARL 65,67. (3) The integration of model learning 
and policy learning in MARL scenarios, a potential iterative process in 
real-world scenarios, has not been achieved65.
Here we surpass the existing research bottleneck and take a sig-
nificant step towards a decision paradigm that is scalable to large-scale 
systems. First, we propose the ξ-dependent networked MDP, which has 
the Markovian property of transition. ξ  characterizes the difference 
between the decoupled system dynamics and the real system dynamics. 
This is a form of decentralized partially observable MDP, and its advan-
tage lies in its ability to deal with more general partially observable 
cooperative multi-agent tasks. This concept bridges the gap between 
standard network systems and general multi-agent systems, providing 
the necessary theoretical framework and analytical tools for research 
into multi-agent systems. Second, building on ξ-dependent network 
systems, we propose a decentralized model-based framework for 
networked systems, called model-based decentralized policy optimiza-
tion. Our method is efficient and scalable, enabling overall perfor -
mance improvement of the general system even when individual agents 
have severely limited access to information retrieval (our method can 
achieve the minimum local message size O (|si|)). 𝑂 is a mathematical 
notation used to describe the asymptotic upper bound of the complex-
ity of an algorithm; 𝑠𝑖 rerepresents the state of agent i. In our method, 
we couple localized model learning with decentralized policy optimiza-
tion. Specifically, we use well-learned localized models to predict future 
states and use local communication to broadcast their predictions. T o 
alleviate the issue of compounding model error, we adopt a branching 
strategy 54,68 by replacing few long-horizon rollouts with many 
short-horizon rollouts to reduce compounding errors in 
model-generated rollouts. In the policy optimization part, we perform 
decentralized proximal policy optimization (PPO)69 using data gener-
ated by localized models and incorporating an extended value function. 
Theoretically, we prove that the policy gradient computed from the 
extended value function is a close approximation to the true gradient, 
establishing the theoretical bridge between model learning and mono-
tonic policy improvement. Our theoretical analysis begins with extend-
ing the fully independent systems 41,70 to more realistic and general 
multi-agent systems, and expanding single-agent model learning 
theory54 to multi-agent systems.
Empirically, we evaluate our algorithms in highly realistic simula-
tors and real-world scenarios in a progressive manner, encompassing 
various systems such as transportation, electric energy and social 
healthcare. Specifically, the evaluation scenarios involved: (1) coop-
erative adaptive cruise control (CACC)21 and connected autonomous 
vehicle control (Flow) 71, (2) adaptive traffic signal control (ATSC), 
including the construction of a traffic network using real maps of 
Monaco21 and New York, (3) IEEE Power Grid72 and the real power sys-
tems constructed using real-time electricity consumption data from 
Portugal73, and (4) the Pandemic Networks constructed by epidemiolo-
gists based on real epidemiological dynamics, COVID-19 prevention 
and control policies, and infection status from real Swedish govern -
ment data74. These tasks accurately and comprehensively simulate the 
complexities and challenges that exist in large-scale networked sys -
tems, with agent counts reaching 199 and 436. Importantly, although 
some MARL methods can handle tasks involving large-scale multi-agent 
systems (500 agents and above)75–77, these approaches often simplify 
the complexity of large-scale problems through agent grouping76,77 or 
mean-field approximations75. The problem setting remains approxi-
mately centralized and cannot address decentralized multi-agent 
learning in communication-limited scenarios. In contrast, our method 
is decentralized. Therefore, it is necessary to qualify our statements to 
indicate that our method significantly surpasses previous decentral-
ized MARL methods in terms of the number of agents it can handle.

Scaling model-based methods to real tasks can lead to decreased 
performance, even if the model itself is relatively accurate. A primary 
reason for this is the compounding modelling error during long model 
rollouts, where errors accumulate along the rollout trajectory, ulti-
mately making it inaccurate. T o mitigate the negative effects of model 
error, we adopt a branched rollout scheme54,68. In branched rollout, 
the model rollout does not start from an initial state, but from a state 
randomly selected from the most recent environmental trajectory τ. 
In addition, the model rollout length is fixed to T. The model training 
steps are described in lines 4–7 of Algorithm 2.
Results
In this section, we introduce the results of our method and baselines 
(more details in Supplementary Table 1) in the control of large-scale 
networked systems (more description in Supplementary Section 2). 
We have solved many control problems in simulators and real-world 
systems (more details in Supplementary Table 2). Baselines consist 
of two main categories: model-based algorithms such as MAG64, inde-
pendent model-based policy optimization (IMPO) and MPO na, and 
model-free algorithms such as CommNet47, IC3Net79, DIAL45, Neur-
Comm21, ConseNet40, FPrint80, CPPO and DPPO, where IMPO is a fully 
independent model-based MARL method, MPOna is an ablated discon-
nected agents in our method (Fig. 1b) and our method with linear mod-
els ablated linear models in our method (more details in Supplementary 
Fig. 1). Overall, our method has decentralized models, policies and value 
functions, with the minimum local message size. Especially compared 
with the centralized methods, the communication costs of our method 
in different scenarios is only about 5–35% of that in centralized methods 
(more details in Supplementary Table 3).
Training results and ablation study
Learning process. We first focus on the convergence of the episode 
reward and sample efficiency in the learning process. Figure 3e shows 
that our method significantly improves sample efficiency and achieves 
the highest sample efficiency, converging within 1 × 10−5 training steps, 
exceedingly outperforms baselines (more results in Supplementary 
Fig. 2). The reason is that the usage of the model will greatly increase 
the amount of experience to support training and improve the explo-
ration efficiency (corresponding to lines 9–12 in Algorithm 3). As seen 
in Fig. 3d, our algorithm generates more extensive state-visited (the 
state-visited point set covers more areas in two-dimensional space), 
further confirming this point. Therefore, the experimental results 
are consistent with the theoretical results. From the results in differ-
ent tasks, we observe that the performance of CPPO does not exceed 
that of the algorithms that use extended value functions. In this way, 
we conclude that by using an extended value function, a centralized 
algorithm can be decomposed into a decentralized algorithm, but the 
performance would not drop significantly. According to Theorems 3 
and 4, the extended value function is accurate enough to evaluate the 
overall multi-agent system and support the training process. Therefore, 
the experimental results are consistent with the theoretical results. 
Compared with the two model-based baselines IMPO and MPO na, we 
conclude that the existence of the extended value increased the esti-
mation accuracy of the global value function and gradient. Although 
theoretical analysis of our method in Theorems 3 and 4 does not pro-
vide more restrictions on whether the neighbour selection is adjacent, 
in the actual experiment we recommend using information of adjacent 
neighbours in the topology of the networked systems to predict the 
global value and gradient, which can reduce the variance of the learn-
ing process and improve the performance. It is worth noting that MAG 
models the learning process of model errors as MARL tasks, resulting 
in lower model errors in certain scenarios. However, MAG still relies on 
global modelling information during the learning phase. In the model 
prediction phase, the traditional control algorithm MPC is utilized 
in the model prediction phase, thus having a higher computational 
complexity and communication cost.
In terms of optimal performance, Fig. 3a shows that our method 
reaches the highest performance with the minimum local message 
size among all the baselines. Due to the fact that CPPO is a fully central-
ized algorithm, where each agent obtains global complete informa -
tion, it has the highest theoretical performance and performs well on 
ATSC-Monaco. However, due to its dependence on large network size 
and computing resources, it is difficult to truly achieve theoretical 
optimal performance. MAG also has high performance in multiple 
scenarios but leads to high computational burden and poor scalability.
Rollout length. T o verify the relationship of rollout length k to Theorems 
1 and 2, we designed relevant experiments to observe the effects of differ-
ent choices of rollout length on the convergence of the episode reward 
and sample efficiency. Model rollout length represents the step size of 
using the model forward to predict the state and reward. Figure 3b shows 
more details of the experimental results. We choose four representative 
environments: CACC Catch-up, Ring Attenuation, ATSC-Monaco and 
Power Grid with the set of rollout lengths 1, 25, 50, 100 and 200, to validate 
how sensitive the accuracy of environment model is to rollout length. 
From the final episode reward and sample efficiency in Ring Attenua-
tion under the rollout lengths of 1, 25 and 100, it can be concluded that 
the environment model is accurate enough to support normal training. 
However, the accuracy of model drops significantly when the rollout 
length is 200, which is reflected in the final episode reward and sample 
efficiency being lower than for shorter rollout lengths. According to 
Theorems 1 and 2, a shorter rollout length k leads to a lower model error 
and better convergence of episode reward. Therefore, the experimental 
results are consistent with the theoretical results.

Discussion
Here we provide a theoretical framework for scalable decision-making 
and propose a MARL framework for the control of general systems. 
Notably, our MARL framework remains effective even when the system 
size scales up to the hundreds of agents, which significantly surpasses 
the scale of problems addressed by previous decentralized methods. 
Therefore, this method holds promise in establishing large-scale AI 
decision-makers for traffic, energy systems and pandemic systems, 
thereby elevating the intelligence level in the real world. The core idea 
in this work shares an intriguing parallel with the theory of six degrees 
of separation82. Through interconnected topology, agents require 
only minimal information exchange to assess the global situation. In 
the future, we hope to explore the optimal system topology through 
information entropy theory and expand modules such as vision and 
natural language, which will comprehensively improve the ability of AI 
models and provide insights for developing scalable AI methodologies.

Monotonic model-based improvement
In model-based learning, different rollout schemes can be chosen. The 
vanilla rollout assumes that models are used in an infinite horizon. The 
branched rollout performs a rollout from a state sampled by a state 
distribution of previous policy πD, and runs T steps in ̂π according to π. 
Based on different rollout schemes, we can construct two lower bounds.

Compared with the theorems of model-based policy optimiza -
tion in the single-agent domain54, we extend them to the multi-agent 
domain, especially for completely independent networked systems 
or even more general and real-world systems named ξ -dependent 
networked systems. Further, to solve the problems of the increase of 
computation complexity, communication cost and system instability 
caused by the multi-agent system, we propose a novel communica -
tion mechanism and analyse the accuracy of the estimation of value 
functions and policy gradients through the following theoretical 
proofs, which is completely different from the general model-based 
algorithms.
Therefore, the challenges for our algorithm and theorems are 
mainly twofold in these ξ -dependent networked systems with fewer 
communications. One is that local communication and approxima-
tion dependency bias D(p ∥ ̄p) between independent networked sys-
tems and ξ -dependent networked systems will increase the 
generalization error ϵmi of the environment model and policy shift ϵπi 
between π and π D, which will further increase the lower bounds

fully prove that our method can reduce the influence of these  
errors on the monotonic improvement of the model-based learning 
through the following algorithm design and hyperparameter selec-
tion. The other is that our method needs to provide accurate  
policy gradients and value functions for the multi-agent system to 
guide the policy optimization, which is explained in more detail in 
Theorems 3 and 4.

1 Comparison with Existing Work
We have comprehensively analyzed the relevant work in the field and added more baselines. Our baselines consist of two main
categories: model-based algorithms such as MAG1 and model-free algorithms. In addition, we add some algorithms which are
the ablation of our method on different components such as extended value functions, adjacent information, and local models.
Next, we will describe the existing work and ablation separately.
1.1 Model-based learning in multi-agent systems
In the work about model-based multi-agent reinforcement learning, we focused on analyzing the following works and compared
MAG as one of the baselines through experiments.
• MAG1 , a multi-agent model optimization framework, adopts a novel approach whereby local models are conceptualized
as multi-step decision-making agents, and the current policies are viewed as the dynamics shaping the model rollout
process. This unique perspective enables the local models to incorporate the intricate interplay and mutual influence
across multiple time steps, offering a more holistic consideration before generating predictions. But MAG uses global
information that has been processed by the Attention block as the input of the critic. This is still essentially a centralized
training method, only using local models, while in our method, the models, polices, and critics are all decentralized.
• Furthermore, some exist model-based multi-agent reinforcement learning endeavors that concentrate on goal alignment
within the context of navigation ensemble tasks2. These works presuppose the availability of pre-trained peer-to-peer
navigation policies and models. They construct a modular system composed of learning components. However, they do
not delve into the specifics of model learning theory or policy improvement theory.
• In certain model-based multi-agent reinforcement learning endeavors, communication and interaction mechanisms are
also employed3. Agents engage in iterative exchanges of their individual forecasts to collaboratively plan ahead. Each
agent has access to a learned world model capable of generating future state and reward predictions conditioned on
actions sampled from the agent’s policy. Subsequently, these insights are encoded into messages and employed in training
through differentiable message passing to learn communication protocols. However, this approach does not take into
account scenarios with constrained communication, rendering it unsuitable for large-scale real-world systems subject to
objective communication restrictions. And there is no theoretical joint analysis of model learning and strategy learning
errors.
• Some researchers have also proposed an Intention Sharing (IS) mechanism 4 to enhance the coordination among agents.
In this scheme, each agent employs an attention mechanism based on messages received from other agents to learn the
relative significance of components within imagined trajectories. This compression allows for capturing the agent’s
future action plans in the form of an intent message for communication. Nevertheless, this method still disregards
communication limitations prevalent in the real world. Additionally, it necessitates extra information learning, thereby
introducing additional computational costs.
In a comprehensive assessment, the existing model-based approaches for multi-agent reinforcement learning suffer from
the following issues: (1) Despite being rooted in local models, they still rely on a global critic, rendering them ineffective
in communication-constrained scenarios. (2) Theoretical analysis and bounded estimation for multi-agent model learning
are lacking, resulting in an insufficient grasp of the model’s impact on reinforcement learning. (3) The integration of model
learning and policy learning, a potential iterative process in real-world scenarios, has not been achieved.

may even increase decision errors. Certain studies have directed their attention towards communication strategies that
involve the selective transmission of messages. For instance, ATOC11 employs a soft attention mechanism to apportion a
communication probability to individual agents, whereas IC3Net10 adopts a hard binary attention approach to determine
whether communication should occur. In IC3Net, the agents maintain their local hidden states with a LSTM kernel.

important to note that these approaches find heightened relevance in scenarios where the necessity for communication
target prioritization is prominent. However, in the context of Scenarios with limited communication, this need is less
pronounced, as the communication scope is typically confined to localized neighborhoods. This method still uses global
information.
• Non-hidden information-based algorithms ConseNet and FPrint exclusively rely on neighborhood information. In the
case of ConseNet, an additional consensus update mechanism is employed, facilitating the overwrite of critic parameters
with the mean values obtained from all critics within the enclosed neighborhood. On the other hand, FPrint incorporates
the inclusion of neighbor policies as an integral component of its framework. It is worth noting that in real scenarios,
the difficulty for intelligent agents to obtain their neighbor policies is often larger than that of obtaining neighbor states
through simple sensors. Our algorithm also does not require neighbor policy information.
1.3 Other ablation baselines
The following algorithm is the ablation of our method on different components such as extended value functions, adjacent
information, and local models.
• CPPO 12, 13: Centralized PPO learns a centralized critic Vi(s). This baseline aims to analyze the performance when κ
is set to be arbitrarily huge, and is used in13 as a benchmark algorithm for networked system control. The comparison
between the performance of CPPO and our method can be viewed as an ablation study of extended value function and
model usage.
• DPPO 12: Decentralized PPO learns an independent actor and critic for each agent. We implement it by using the
neighbor’s state for extended value estimation. The comparison between the performance of DPPO and our method can
be viewed as an ablation study of model usage.
• IMPO: A decentralized and model-based algorithm based on our method, but without information of neighbors and
does not use the extended value function to predict global value function and gradient. The comparison between the
performance of IMPO and our method can be viewed as an ablation study of the extended value function.
• MPOna: A decentralized and model-based algorithm based on our method, but without information of non-adjacent
neighbors. The comparison between the performance of MPOna and our method can be viewed as an ablation study of
adjacent neighbors. The more detailed description is shown in Fig. 1 in the main text.
1.4 Theoretical connections with previous work
In addition to the aforementioned aspects, prior work has introduced the foundational assumption of completely independent
systems14. However, we believe that this assumption is considerably strong and not well-suited for the intricacies of real-world
complex systems. Algorithms designed under such an assumption lack scalability, rendering them less viable for large-scale and
intricate systems. As the number of agents grows, the approximation errors introduced by this assumption become challenging
to control. Furthermore, due to the absence of model learning, these algorithms exhibit low exploration efficiency and sample
efficiency in complex tasks. Rapidly attaining optimal policies becomes arduous. To address these limitations, we have

scales and a wider array of intricate tasks. Although our theoretical findings are based on the work of Scalable SAC 14, we

distributed generators, which can be formulated as a cooperative MARL problem. The objective of Power-Grid is to maintain
the voltage and frequency of all distributed generators to the reference value when the disruptions in the systems and tracking

values for each load are added in each simulation time step.

The Real-world Active Voltage ControlTo further validate the efficacy of our method in real-world scenarios, we tackle
the challenge of active control in a large-scale power system 16 constructed with real-world data. The emerging trend of
decarbonization is placing excessive stress on power distribution networks. Active voltage control is seen as a promising
solution to relieve power congestion and improve voltage quality without extra hardware investment, taking advantage of the
controllable apparatuses in the network, such as roof-top photovoltaics (PVs) and static var compensators (SVCs). These
controllable apparatuses appear in a vast number and are distributed in a wide geographic area, making MARL a natural
candidate. These power systems consist of 141 and 322 buses, respectively. To facilitate operational efficiency, a large-scale
power network is partitioned into multiple regions, with each region being equipped with several photovoltaic (PV) installations
overseen by the respective distribution network owner. Within this setup, each PV is equipped with an inverter that generates
reactive power to regulate the voltage around a fixed reference value denoted asvref. In our task, we assume the independence
and segregation of PV ownership (e.g., individual homeowners or enterprises) from distribution network ownership. Notably,
the operation of PV infrastructure falls under the responsibility of the PV owner. This implies that each PV can be controlled
in a distributed manner, naturally lending itself to the characterization of an agent. The objective is to ensure that the power
systems is within safe control range and has minimal power losses. The load profiles of each electrical network are derived
from real-time Portuguese electricity consumption data spanning a period of three years, encompassing 232 consumers. In
order to highlight the distinctions between residential and industrial users, the default power factors specified in the case files
are randomly perturbed by±5%, thereby generating corresponding real-time reactive power consumption. Solar data, sourced
from Elia group, the power network operator in Belgium, is obtained and incorporated. Both the load and photovoltaic (PV)
data are interpolated using a resolution of 3 minutes to align with the real-time control period within the power grid. To
differentiate between varying solar radiation levels across different regions, the PV generation profiles from 10 cities/regions
over a three-year period are collected, with PV systems within the same control region sharing identical generation profiles16.

The active voltage control problem exhibits several intriguing characteristics. Firstly, it manifests as a convergence of
both local and global factors. This denotes that while the voltage of each node is influenced by the real and reactive powers
of all other nodes, the influence diminishes as the distance between nodes increases. Secondly, it constitutes a constrained
optimization quandary, with the voltage threshold serving as the constraint and the minimization of total power loss standing
as the objective. However, the relationship between the constraint and the control action lacks explicit definition. Thirdly,
the structure of a distribution network adheres to a radial topology, offering substantial underlying information for control
strategies. Nevertheless, the node-branch parameters of this topology can introduce significant uncertainties. Lastly, voltage
control possesses a relatively considerable margin of tolerance, resulting in less severe consequences in cases where the control
fails to meet standard requirements. This particular characteristic renders it highly suitable as a practical application scenario
for multi-agent reinforcement learning that relies on a certain degree of exploration and trial-and-error. It serves as an ideal
context for the implementation of grounded multi-agent reinforcement learning applications.

Next, we provide some details of the model learning methods in our method. The universal model error corresponds to the
term D(p∥ ˆp) in the inequality (6) in our manuscript. The model error D(p∥ ˆp) can be divided into two parts: dependency bias
D(p∥ ¯p) and independence-approximation error D( ¯p∥ ˆp). When our model accurately fits the true MDP, we have D( ¯p∥ ˆp)≈ 0,
and supD(p∥ ˆp)≈ supD(p∥ ¯p)≤ ξ . Therefore, εmi≤ D(p∥ ˆp)≤ ξ . We have controlled the model error εmi (TV distance
between the distributions) under ξ bound through model learning to adapt to our analysis of the monotonic improvement
theorem of policy. To obtain a more accurate fitting model, we optimized it from three perspectives: model architecture design,
training methods, and training data.
• Structure of the model We have undertaken numerous design considerations based on practical engineering experience.
Firstly, we meticulously designed the model architecture. Figure 9 in the main text illustrates the overall structure of our
method, wherein the red-bordered box represents the model employed for fitting real-world environments. Concerning
the design of the model framework, we distinctively handle state and action information. They are individually subjected
to feature extraction using embedding layers, followed by amalgamation based on the topological relationships of the
graph network. On one hand, this graph network architecture inherently approximates networked Markov Decision
Processes (MDPs). On the other hand, the separate extraction and aggregation of features facilitate rapid learning of
environmental dynamical traits by the model. This enables us to maintain a relatively compact network size with reduced
model error during training, thereby expediting monotonic policy improvement and minimizing extraneous computational
overhead beyond policy learning.
• Training method and data We employ a supervised learning approach involving multi-step model extrapolation,
iteratively executed in multiple batches. Throughout the model learning process, virtual data generated by the model and
authentic environmental data are continually blended in accordance with a predetermined ratio. During the initial stages
of training, when the model’s accuracy is still developing, the model predominantly relies on authentic environmental
data for supervised learning, effectively performing a warm start. As the model attains a certain level of accuracy, we
progressively elevate the proportion of model-generated data within the overall training dataset. This ensures that the
model effectively fits both mixed real and virtual data. Additionally, guided by disparities between real and virtual
data within the amalgamated dataset, a self-supervised mechanism is employed to further adjust model parameters
during training iterations. This iterative process facilitates the enhancement of model accuracy and maintains a degree
of generalization. During the continuous training of the model, our strategy has also been continuously trained and
improved, and our model fitting and strategy improvement are alternating.

5 Description of Supplementary Videos
5.1 Execution results in ATSC
We show the execution results of ATSC-Grid in our method (Supplementary Video 1) and DPPO (Supplementary Video 2).
Through the key indicator "Insertion-backlogged vehicles" in the numerical panel on the left, we can observe that "Insertion-
backlogged vehicles" will gradually increase with the load of the traffic flow, and then gradually decrease with the effective
decision of the traffic light. The maximum value of this indicator is 1486 vehicles in DPPO and 1033 vehicles in our algorithm,
which indicates that our approach can reduce the backlog of vehicles at intersections. Therefore, compared with DPPO, our
framework is more effective in solving traffic jams. Through the three intersections we focused on in the video, we can also
observe that our method can make traffic jams last for less time.
We further show a more challenging scenario with a heterogeneous network structure with diverse action and observation
spaces: ATSC-Monaco traffic network with 28 intersections of real traffic network in Monaco city. We compare our method
(Supplementary Video 3) with DPPO (Supplementary Video 4). Through the key indicator "arrived vehicles" in the numerical
panel on the left, we can observe that " arrived vehicles " will gradually increase with the load of the traffic flow. The maximum
value of this indicator is 563 vehicles in DPPO and 752 vehicles in our framework, which indicates that our method can reduce
the backlog of vehicles at intersections and allow more vehicles to arrive at their destinations. Therefore, compared with DPPO,
our algorithm is more effective in solving traffic jams. Through the three intersections, we focused on in the video, we can also
observe that our method can make traffic jams last for less time.
5.2 Execution results in Flow
Supplementary Video 5 shows the execution result in Figure Eight. We can observe that our method controls the formation of
queues to cross intersections with an orderly process of accelerating to the target velocity and then decelerating to the safe
velocity near 0 m/s. We conclude that our models control vehicles to obey traffic rules while improving the efficiency of the
overall traffic flow.
Supplementary Video 6 shows the execution result in Ring Attenuation. We can observe that our method can maintain the
stability of the ring traffic flow and improve the efficiency of the overall traffic flow.
5.3 Execution results in Real Power-Net
Supplementary Video 7 shows the performance of the four algorithms on Real Power-Net with 141-bus, with particular
emphasis on the total power loss. The observation period spans 12 hours, and for the sake of presentation, temporal augmentation
has been applied to the video footage. The upper left, upper right, lower left, and lower right corners sequentially showcase
the four algorithms: our method, CPPO, DPPO, and IC3Net. Through the video visualization, it becomes evident that our
algorithm maintains the lowest aggregate power loss. The dynamic variation in colors of the power grid’s structure signifies the
dynamic changes in load, while the bar chart on the right provides a color scale corresponding to different loads; darker colors
indicate higher load levels.
Supplementary Video 8 shows the performance of the four algorithms on Real Power-Net with 322-bus and more complex
topology, with particular emphasis on the total power loss. The observation period spans 12 hours, and for the sake of
presentation, temporal augmentation has been applied to the video footage. The upper left, upper right, lower left, and
lower right corners sequentially showcase the four algorithms: our approach, CPPO, DPPO, and IC3Net. Through the video
visualization, it becomes evident that our method maintains the lowest aggregate power loss. The dynamic variation in colors
of the power grid’s structure signifies the dynamic changes in load, while the bar chart on the right provides a color scale
corresponding to different loads; darker colors indicate higher load levels.
